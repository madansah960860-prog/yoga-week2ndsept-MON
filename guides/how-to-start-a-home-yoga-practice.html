How to start a home yoga practice without buying anything

The gap between deciding to practice at home and actually practicing at home is almost never about equipment. It is about a set of small unresolved questions: where do I do this, what do I do, how long for, and how do I know if I am doing it right. Here are the answers.

The four things you already have

Before anything else, an inventory. A home practice needs four things, and you almost certainly own all of them.

A floor

About 6 feet by 3 feet, enough to lie down with your arms slightly out from your sides. If you have carpet, you have a workable surface with grip and a little padding, and you can practice for a month without owning a mat. If you have hardwood or tile, the floor is slippery under bare hands and feet, which matters in exactly two of the six poses a beginner starts with. A rug or a folded blanket under your hands solves it in the short term.

Clear the space before each session. Not for atmosphere, but because you will be moving backward with your eyes closed at some point and a side table has more edges than you remember.

A wall

The wall is the most under-used free prop in yoga. Four feet of clear wall, from the floor upward, gives you four distinct things.

Standing with your back to it, heels a couple of inches out, you get a straight reference line. Let the back of the pelvis, the shoulder blades and the back of the head make light contact, and you immediately discover where your spine actually sits, which is usually not where you assumed. If the head does not reach the wall comfortably, leave it forward. That is information about your upper back, not a fault to correct by force.

Standing beside it with one hand on it, any balance becomes something you can study rather than something you survive. Tree Pose practiced next to a wall teaches you far more than Tree Pose practiced in the middle of a room with your arms flapping.

Standing facing it with both hands at hip height, you get a version of Downward-Facing Dog with the full shoulder action and almost no weight through the wrists. For anyone whose wrists object to the floor version, this is not a preparation, it is the version.

Lying with your legs up it, you get Viparīta Karaṇī, a supported shape that requires no strength at all and is one of the few things in yoga that most people find immediately pleasant.

A chair

Any sturdy chair without wheels. A dining chair. Its first job is to raise the floor to waist height so that a standing forward fold becomes available without reaching anywhere near the ground. Its second job is to support your thigh in a lunge or a warrior shape so the leg is not doing all the work. Its third job is to be the entire practice, if getting down to and up from the floor is difficult or unsafe for you.

That third use deserves emphasis. Our Desk Reset sequence is eight minutes long, done entirely seated, and covers spinal flexion, extension, side bending and rotation in both directions, plus both hips and the shoulders. That is a complete practice by any reasonable definition. It is not a lesser version of something else.

Ten minutes

Not thirty. Ten. The single most common way a home practice ends is that someone decides it should be forty-five minutes, protects that slot twice, misses it on the third day, and concludes they are not a person who does yoga.

Ten minutes is short enough that you will do it on a bad day, which is the only property that matters in the first month. Length can come later and will come by itself once the habit exists.

What a mat actually changes

Mats are the default first purchase and they are less transformative than the marketing suggests. A mat does two things: it gives your hands and feet grip on a smooth surface, and it provides a modest amount of padding. That is it.

If you are on carpet, you have grip and padding already. Buy a mat when you find you want a defined space to work in, when you start practicing somewhere with a hard floor, or when the carpet becomes annoying, and not before. When you do buy one, roughly 4 to 5 millimeters, about 3/16 of an inch, is the standard thickness and suits most practice. If you are taller than 5 feet 8 inches, look for a 72 inch mat rather than the standard 68 inch.

What is worth buying earlier, if you buy anything, is a pair of foam blocks. Blocks change more poses more usefully than a mat does, because raising the floor toward your hands is the single most common thing a beginner shape needs. Our props guide goes through this in detail.

Choosing what to actually do

The second unresolved question, and the one that stops most people at the moment they have cleared a space and are standing in it. The answer is: pick one short sequence and repeat it.

Repetition is not a lack of ambition. It is how a set of written instructions becomes something your body recognizes. The second week of the same sequence teaches you considerably more than the first week of a new one, because by then you are no longer reading, you are noticing. You find out that your right hip does something different from your left. You find out that the third pose is harder on Thursdays. None of that is available while you are still checking what comes next.

Our 10-Minute Morning sequence is built for this. Fourteen holds, a fixed order, a timer on the page so you are not watching a clock. Read the order once before your first attempt so you are not stopping to look, then run it three times a week for a month.

How to know whether you are doing it right

Three checks, in order, and they cover almost everything.

  1. Can you breathe evenly? If you are holding your breath or breathing raggedly, you have gone past a useful edge. Ease off until the breath is steady. This one check does more work than any alignment cue.
  2. Is the sensation spread out? A broad feeling across a muscle is what you are looking for. Sharp, pinching, or radiating sensation in one small point is a signal to come out, not to breathe into.
  3. Could you come out under control? If leaving a shape would involve collapsing out of it, you are further in than you should be.

Beyond those three, the honest answer is that a qualified teacher watching you for five minutes will tell you more than any website can. Practicing at home and going to an occasional class is a better combination than either on its own. Our find a teacher page covers what to look for and what to ask before booking.

The first four weeks

A plan that is deliberately unambitious, and designed so that missing a day does not break it.

  • Week one. The 10-Minute Morning, three times, same sequence each time. Read Start Here before the first attempt.
  • Week two. The same, three times, plus one Desk Reset on a day you have been sitting a lot. You are now practicing four times a week without having increased the length of anything.
  • Week three. Three mornings plus one Evening Wind-Down. This is usually the week the shapes stop feeling like instructions.
  • Week four. Two mornings, one evening, and one attempt at the Gentle Full Practice on a day you have half an hour available.

If you miss a day, do the next one. There is nothing to make up and no streak to protect. A practice that tolerates gaps is a practice that lasts years.

The things that quietly stop people

Storage. If your mat and blocks live behind the vacuum in a hall closet, the practice happens on the days you were already going to do it. Keep them within arm's reach of where you practice, in a basket or rolled in a corner, so setup takes thirty seconds.

Cold. You will feel colder in the last three minutes than in the first three, because you have stopped moving. A blanket within reach solves it. A cold floor in January is a real reason a practice quietly ends.

Notifications. Put the phone on do not disturb before you start. A message arriving in minute four ends more sessions than fatigue does.

Other people. Practicing in a shared living room feels strange for about a week and then stops being interesting to anybody, including you.

What to read next

If you have not read it yet, Start Here is the full beginner guide and covers the same ground with more detail on the poses themselves. Practice at Home covers space, surface, light and storage. The pose library has all thirty-two shapes with their names, difficulty and prop options.

And then stop reading and do ten minutes. The reading is the easy part.
