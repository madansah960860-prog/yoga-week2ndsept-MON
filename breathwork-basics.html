Breathwork basics

Prāṇāyāma, pronounced prah-nah-YAH-mah, is the part of yoga that works directly with the breath. This page covers four patterns, describes them as mechanics rather than mystery, and gives you a pacer on screen to breathe along with.

One thing to be clear about before anything else: these are breathing exercises, not treatments. This page makes no claim that any pattern here treats, prevents or improves any condition. If breathing exercises have been recommended to you for a specific reason, follow the guidance of the professional who recommended them rather than a website.

Start with one rule

Before any pattern, there is a single instruction that covers your first month of practice: breathe in and out through the nose, at a pace that stays comfortable, and do not hold your breath when a pose gets demanding.

That last part is the useful bit. Breath-holding under effort is the most reliable signal that you have gone past a productive edge in a pose. If you cannot keep an even nasal breath, ease off until you can. No further instruction is needed for a long time.

Where the breath goes

Put one hand on your lower ribs at the side and one on your belly. Breathe in normally. You are looking for the lower ribs to widen sideways under your hand. Most people, most of the time, breathe higher and shallower than that, particularly when concentrating.

Nothing dramatic needs to change. Noticing where the movement happens is the whole exercise, and the noticing is what carries into the poses.

Breath pacer

The circle grows as you breathe in and shrinks as you breathe out. Pick a pattern, press start, and follow it for a minute or two. If a pattern feels like a strain, go back to even breathing or stop.

Ready

Paused

The pacer is a visual metronome, nothing more. Stop at any point. If you feel dizzy, breathless or uncomfortable, return to your normal breathing immediately.

1. Even breathing

Sama Vṛtti, pronounced SAH-mah VRIT-tee. Inhale and exhale for the same count.

How. Sit comfortably or lie down. Breathe in through the nose for a count of four, out through the nose for a count of four. Keep the count steady and unhurried. Ten to twenty rounds.

What is happening mechanically. You are matching the length of the in-breath and the out-breath, which for most people means slowing the breath rate down from wherever it sits naturally. Nothing is being held.

Where to use it. At the very start of a practice, to settle. In seated shapes. Any time a pattern with holds feels like too much.

Adjusting the count. Four is a starting number, not a rule. If four feels long, use three. The evenness matters more than the number.

2. Extended exhale

Viṣama Vṛtti, an uneven ratio. The out-breath is longer than the in-breath.

How. In through the nose for four, out through the nose for six. Let the exhale be unhurried rather than forced; you are lengthening it, not squeezing the last of the air out. Ten to fifteen rounds.

What is happening mechanically. The out-breath is the passive half of a normal breath cycle, so lengthening it means slowing the release rather than adding effort. Many people find the count easier to hold on the exhale than the inhale for exactly that reason.

Where to use it. In supported resting shapes such as Child's Pose or Legs Up the Wall, and in the long holds of the Evening Wind-Down sequence.

If six is too long. Use four in and five out. Small ratios are still ratios.

3. Box breathing

Four equal parts: in, hold, out, hold.

How. In for four, hold for four, out for four, hold for four. Eight to ten rounds is plenty. The holds should be relaxed pauses, not a clamped throat or a braced chest.

What is happening mechanically. You are adding two pauses of equal length to an even breath. The pauses are what make this pattern more demanding than the first two, and they are also the part people most often force.

Where to use it. Seated, on its own, not during physical shapes.

Important. Breath retention is the one element here that some people should avoid. See the safety note below. If you have any doubt, use even breathing instead, which offers the same slowing without the holds.

4. Slow breathing

A longer version of the extended exhale, five in and seven out.

How. In for five, out for seven. Ten rounds. If the count causes any gasping at the top of the inhale, the count is too long; drop back to four and six.

What is happening mechanically. The breath rate drops to roughly five breaths a minute. That is considerably slower than a typical resting rate, which is why it takes practice to keep it comfortable rather than effortful.

Where to use it. At the end of a practice, in Corpse Pose, or seated on its own.

The test for whether it is working. You should be able to stop at any moment and take a normal breath without feeling like you need to catch up. If you cannot, shorten the counts.

Two patterns worth knowing by name

You will hear these in classes. Neither is on the pacer above, because both are better learned from a teacher watching you than from a screen.

  • Ujjāyī, pronounced oo-JAH-yee. A slight narrowing at the back of the throat that gives the breath an audible, soft sound. Used in vinyasa and Ashtanga classes to keep a rhythm audible across a room. Practiced without strain it is simply a quieter, slower breath; forced, it becomes a throat-gripping habit that is hard to unlearn.
  • Nāḍī Śodhana, pronounced NAH-dee SHOH-dah-nah, alternate nostril breathing. One nostril is closed with the fingers, then the other, alternating through the cycle. Ask a teacher to set up your hand position; it is fiddly to learn from text and easy to do awkwardly.

How much, how often

Two to five minutes is a complete session. There is no benefit to be chased by doing thirty. Once a day, or before a physical practice, or in the final resting shape, is plenty.

Sit somewhere your spine can be upright without effort. A chair with both feet flat is perfectly good and is better than a cross-legged position that has you rounding backward. If sitting upright is tiring, lie down with a cushion under the knees.

Counting without a screen

Once you know a pattern, drop the pacer. Count silently, or use the length of a slow mental phrase instead of numbers. The point of the visual is to teach you the rhythm, not to be needed permanently.

Safety, and when to stop

Stop immediately and return to normal breathing if you feel dizzy, lightheaded, breathless, tingling in the hands or face, chest discomfort, or anxious. These are signs to stop, not signs to push through, and they mean the pattern or the count does not suit you today.

Avoid breath retention, meaning the holds in box breathing, if you are pregnant, have a heart condition, have high or low blood pressure, have a respiratory condition such as asthma or COPD, have glaucoma or raised eye pressure, have a history of seizures, or have a history of panic. Even breathing and extended exhale involve no retention and are the patterns to use instead.

Never practice any breathing exercise in water, while driving, or while standing somewhere you could fall.

Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before beginning breathing exercises, particularly if you are pregnant, recently unwell, or managing a health condition, and work with a qualified teacher in person for guidance.
