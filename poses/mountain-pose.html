Pose guide

Mountain Pose

Tāḍāsana, pronounced tah-DAH-suh-nuh

  • Beginner
  • Standing
  • No props required
  • Hold 30 to 60 seconds

Mountain Pose is standing, done on purpose. It looks like nothing and it is the reference position for every other upright shape in the practice. When a teacher says the front knee should track over the ankle in Warrior Two, the standard being described was set here.

Entry steps

  1. Set the feet. Stand with the feet about hip-width apart and parallel, second toes pointing straight ahead. Feet together is a traditional version and a harder balance; hip-width is the better default.
  2. Find the middle. Rock gently forward onto the balls of the feet, then back toward the heels, then side to side. Shrink the movement until you settle in the middle, with the weight spread across the whole sole.
  3. Release the knees. Straighten the legs without locking them backward. The kneecaps should still be able to move a little if you flex the thigh muscles.
  4. Level the pelvis. Let the pelvis rest over the legs without tucking the tailbone under or tipping it back. The lower back keeps its natural inward curve.
  5. Settle the ribs. Let the front ribs drop toward the waist rather than flaring forward, while the crown of the head reaches up.
  6. Rest the arms. Arms hang beside the body, palms turning gently toward the thighs, shoulders wide rather than pulled back.

Alignment cues

  • Ankles, hips, shoulders, ears. Seen from the side, these four points sit roughly over one another.
  • Even arches. Press the big toe mound, the little toe mound and the heel down equally. The inner arch stays lifted without gripping.
  • Long back of the neck. Chin parallel to the floor, jaw unclenched, tongue soft.
  • Wide collarbones. Broaden across the front of the chest instead of squeezing the shoulder blades together.
  • Quiet effort. The legs are engaged, the face is not.

Breath pattern

  • Breathe in and out through the nose at your own pace. Nothing here is timed to the breath.
  • Let the inhale widen the lower ribs sideways rather than lifting the shoulders.
  • Let the exhale be slightly longer than the inhale, without forcing the end of it.
  • If you notice yourself holding the breath, soften the legs slightly and start again.

Suggested hold

  • First time: 30 seconds, roughly five to eight breaths.
  • Regular practice: 60 seconds at the start of a standing sequence.
  • Between standing poses: 3 to 5 breaths to reset before the next shape.

Common mistakes

  • Locking the knees back. Pushing the knees hard backward shifts the pelvis forward. Micro-bend, then straighten to just short of the end range.
  • Standing on the heels. Most people default backward. Bring a little weight into the front of the foot until all four corners share the load.
  • Military chest. Pulling the shoulders back and lifting the chest flares the front ribs. Let the ribs come down and the width happen across the collarbones instead.
  • Chin lifting. Reaching up through the crown often drags the chin up. Keep the chin level and the length in the back of the neck.
  • Gripping the toes. Curled toes are usually a balance strategy. Spread them and let the whole foot do the work.

What is actually happening

  • The muscles around the ankles, knees and hips make small continuous corrections to keep you upright. That constant low-level work is the point of the shape.
  • The deep muscles alongside the spine hold the natural curves of the lower back, mid back and neck.
  • Because you are not moving, you get a clear reading of the differences between your left and right sides, which is the most useful information the pose offers.

Modifications

These are alternative versions of the same pose, not lesser ones. Choose whichever gives you the clearest information about your own standing.

Wall behind you

Stand with the heels about two inches from a wall and let the back of the pelvis, the shoulder blades and the back of the head make light contact. The wall gives you a straight reference line so you can feel where your spine actually is rather than where you assume it is. If the head does not reach the wall comfortably, leave it forward. That is information, not a failure.

Seated in a chair

Sit toward the front of a sturdy chair with both feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart, knees over ankles. Build the same shape upward from the sit bones: level pelvis, ribs settled, crown reaching. Every alignment cue above applies except the ones about the feet carrying weight. This is the version to use if standing for a minute is uncomfortable or unsafe.

Feet wider, hands on a chair back

Take the feet a little wider than hip-width and rest the fingertips lightly on the back of a chair placed in front of you. The wider base and the light hand contact remove the balance demand entirely, which lets you concentrate on the leg and spine cues.

Block between the thighs

Hold a foam block lengthwise between the upper inner thighs and squeeze it gently. This gives the inner leg muscles something to do and often makes it much easier to feel the pelvis settle level. Useful if the legs feel vague or if you tend to roll onto the outer edges of the feet.

Progression

Once standing for a minute is uneventful, the shape develops in three directions.

  • Arms overhead, Ūrdhva Hastāsana. Keep everything below the ribs exactly as it was and sweep the arms out and up on an inhale, palms facing each other. The test is whether the front ribs stay down.
  • Eyes closed. Removing vision makes the ankles and hips work considerably harder. Stand near a wall the first few times.
  • Feet together. Bring the big toes to touch with a small gap between the heels. A narrower base, a harder balance, and the traditional version of the pose.

From here the natural next shapes are Tree Pose, which takes the same standing base onto one leg, and Warrior Two, which widens it.

Contraindications and care

Approach with care or skip this pose if: you have low blood pressure or get lightheaded when standing still, you have a balance disorder or a history of falls, you are recovering from a recent lower-limb or spinal injury or surgery, or standing for a minute at a time is uncomfortable for any reason. In every one of those cases the seated chair version above gives you the same practice.

If you become dizzy, sit down. If you have any condition affecting balance, blood pressure or the spine, speak with a qualified healthcare professional before practicing, and work with a qualified teacher in person who can watch you and give hands-on guidance.
