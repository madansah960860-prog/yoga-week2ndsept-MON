Pose guide

Downward-Facing Dog

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana, pronounced AH-doh MOO-kah shvah-NAH-suh-nuh

  • Beginner
  • Standing and arm supported
  • Blocks or a wall help
  • Hold 30 to 60 seconds

An inverted V with the hands and feet on the floor and the hips at the high point. It appears in almost every flowing class, often as a resting shape, which is a joke to anyone practicing it for the first time. Done with bent knees it is genuinely restful. Done with forced straight legs it is not.

Entry steps

  1. Start on hands and knees. Hands under the shoulders, knees under the hips. Spread the fingers wide and point the middle fingers straight ahead.
  2. Walk the hands forward. Move the hands about one hand-length in front of the shoulders. This small step is what stops the pose from collapsing into the wrists later.
  3. Tuck the toes. Curl the toes under and press the balls of the feet down.
  4. Lift the hips with bent knees. Press the whole hand into the floor and lift the hips up and back. Keep the knees deeply bent, more than feels necessary.
  5. Reach the sit bones up. Aim the sit bones toward the ceiling. The spine should feel long from the hands all the way to the hips.
  6. Let the head hang. Ears in line with the upper arms, neck released, gaze toward the thighs or the floor between the feet.
  7. Straighten only as far as the spine allows. Send the heels down a little at a time. The instant the lower back starts to round, bend the knees again. Bent knees are the correct version for most people.

Alignment cues

  • Whole hand, not just the heel of the hand. Press the knuckle at the base of the index finger down firmly. It takes load off the outer wrist.
  • Elbow creases forward. Turn the inner elbows to face each other, which externally rotates the upper arms and gives the shoulders room.
  • Long spine over straight legs. If you have to choose, choose the long spine.
  • Feet hip-width, toes forward. Heels can be slightly wider than the toes.
  • Even weight. Roughly sixty percent of the weight in the legs, forty in the arms. If the arms are screaming, walk the feet in a few inches.

Breath pattern

  • Enter the shape on an exhale as the hips lift.
  • Once there, breathe evenly through the nose. Do not try to lengthen the breath in this pose while you are new to it.
  • Let each exhale be a moment where the shoulders soften rather than a moment where you push deeper.
  • Come down on an exhale, knees to the floor first.

Suggested hold

  • First time: 15 to 30 seconds, then rest in Child's Pose.
  • Regular practice: 30 to 60 seconds, or five to eight breaths.
  • In a flowing sequence: 3 to 5 breaths between other shapes.

Common mistakes

  • Straightening the legs at the cost of the spine. The most common error by a wide margin. A rounded lower back with straight legs is a worse version of this pose than bent knees with a long back.
  • Hands too close to the feet. Makes the shape steep, dumps weight into the wrists and jams the shoulders. Walk the hands forward.
  • Chasing the heels to the floor. Whether your heels touch depends largely on ankle range and limb proportion. It is not a measure of progress.
  • Gripping with the fingers. Clawing the mat usually means the weight has slid into the wrist heel. Spread and press the whole hand instead.
  • Head lifted, neck cranked. Looking forward compresses the back of the neck. Let the head hang.
  • Holding on too long. Two shorter, well-built holds are worth more than one long collapsing one.

What is actually happening

  • The shoulders move into deep flexion, with the muscles around the shoulder blades working to keep the joint stable while the arms carry load.
  • The muscles along the back of the legs, the calves and hamstrings, are held at length while the hips fold.
  • The wrists take weight in extension, which is the reason this pose needs building up to rather than jumping into daily.
  • The muscles down the length of the spine work to keep the back long against gravity.

Modifications

Every version below produces the same shoulder and leg action. Pick the one that lets you keep a long spine and an even breath.

Hands on a wall

Stand about three feet from a wall, place both palms on it at hip height, walk the feet back and let the torso come down toward horizontal so the body makes an L. You get the full shoulder action and the long spine with almost no weight in the wrists. This is the best starting version for anyone with wrist sensitivity, and it is a legitimate practice on its own, not a stepping stone.

Hands on a chair seat

Place a sturdy chair against a wall so it cannot slide. Put both hands on the seat, walk the feet back and lower the torso toward horizontal. The chair sits between the wall version and the floor version in terms of load, and it lets you fold at the hips more deeply than the wall allows.

Blocks under the hands

Set two blocks at their medium height about shoulder-width apart and place a hand on each. Raising the hands reduces the angle at the wrists and gives a taller person more room to lengthen the spine. Blocks with a flat, wide face work better than rounded ones here.

Forearms down, Dolphin

Come onto the forearms with the elbows shoulder-width apart and the hands either parallel or clasped. This removes the wrists from the equation completely while asking more from the shoulders. Keep the knees well bent.

Knees down between rounds

Rather than holding one long attempt, do three rounds of ten seconds with the knees down on the floor in between. Short repeated exposure builds tolerance in the wrists and shoulders far more comfortably than a single long hold.

Strap around the upper arms

Loop a strap around both upper arms just above the elbows, set to shoulder width, and press outward into it. It stops the elbows from splaying wide, which is a common reason the shoulders feel unstable in this shape.

Progression

  • Three-legged dog. From a steady shape, lift one leg straight back and up, keeping both hips level. Adds a single-arm-dominant load without changing the base.
  • Dog to plank and back. Shift forward into Plank Pose on an inhale and back to the dog on an exhale. Five rounds. This is the honest strength builder for the shoulders.
  • Longer holds. Work from 30 seconds toward 90 seconds over several weeks before adding anything else.

This pose sits inside the Gentle Full Practice sequence, where it is entered from hands and knees and followed by a rest.

Contraindications and care

Approach with care or choose a wall or chair version if: you have wrist pain, carpal tunnel symptoms, a recent wrist, elbow or shoulder injury, a shoulder that dislocates, high blood pressure that is not well controlled, glaucoma or other conditions where a head-below-heart position has been discouraged for you, or you are in later pregnancy when the shape becomes awkward rather than unsafe.

Skip it entirely and speak with a professional first if: you are recovering from surgery to the wrist, shoulder, abdomen or spine, or you have been told to avoid weight-bearing through the arms.
