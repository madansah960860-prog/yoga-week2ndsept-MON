Pose guide

Warrior Two

Vīrabhadrāsana II, pronounced veer-ah-bah-DRAH-suh-nuh

  • Beginner
  • Standing
  • Chair option
  • Hold 30 to 60 seconds each side

A wide stance with one knee bent, both arms reaching horizontally and the gaze past the front hand. It is one of the first poses that asks the front thigh to work for a sustained period, which is why the legs often start shaking before the shape is technically wrong. Shaking is fatigue, not a mistake.

Entry steps

  1. Stand facing the long edge. From Mountain Pose, turn to face the long side of your mat or the open room.
  2. Step wide. Step the feet three to four feet apart. Taller people need more, shorter people less. The wrists should end up roughly above the ankles when the arms are open.
  3. Turn the feet. Turn the right foot out ninety degrees so the toes point along the mat. Turn the left toes in about fifteen degrees.
  4. Line up the heels. The right heel lines up with the arch of the left foot. If balance feels precarious, step the back foot a few inches wider than that line and it becomes far steadier.
  5. Bend the front knee. On an exhale, bend the right knee toward ninety degrees. Stop wherever the knee is still stacked above or behind the ankle, never in front of it.
  6. Open the arms. Reach both arms out at shoulder height, palms down, shoulder blades wide. Let the shoulders drop away from the ears.
  7. Turn the head. Turn to look past the front hand, keeping the neck long. If the neck complains, keep looking straight ahead instead.

Alignment cues

  • Front knee over the second toe. The knee tends to drift inward. Press the outer edge of the front foot down and think of the knee moving toward the little toe side.
  • Back foot grounded. The outer edge of the back foot presses down. If that heel keeps lifting, shorten the stance.
  • Torso upright. Shoulders stack over the hips, not leaning toward the front leg. Most people lean forward without noticing.
  • Arms at shoulder height. Not higher. Lifting the arms above shoulder height tenses the neck.
  • Ribs down. Keep the front ribs from flaring forward as the arms open.

Breath pattern

  • Step wide on an inhale, bend the front knee on an exhale.
  • Hold with an even nasal breath. Do not lengthen or hold the breath here.
  • Use each exhale to soften the jaw and the shoulders while the legs keep working.
  • Straighten the front leg on an inhale to come out.

Suggested hold

  • First time: 20 to 30 seconds each side.
  • Regular practice: 45 to 60 seconds each side, or six to ten breaths.
  • Building leg endurance: two rounds of 30 seconds per side with a rest between.

Common mistakes

  • Front knee collapsing inward. The single most important thing to fix. Widen the stance slightly and press the outer front foot down.
  • Knee past the ankle. Usually caused by a stance that is too short. Step the back foot further back rather than reducing the bend.
  • Leaning over the front leg. The torso should stay upright between the two feet. Check by imagining a plumb line from the top of the head to the floor between your feet.
  • Shoulders creeping up. Reach the arms wide, then let the shoulders drop while the fingertips keep reaching.
  • Back ribs arching. Opening the arms often pulls the front ribs forward. Let them settle.
  • Holding a shape you are fighting. If the breath goes ragged, straighten the front leg partway. A higher stance held well is worth more than a deep one held badly.

What is actually happening

  • The front thigh muscles work hard in a lengthened position to hold the bent knee, which is why they fatigue quickly.
  • The muscles of the outer hip on the front leg work to keep the knee tracking outward against the pull of the inner thigh.
  • The back leg's inner thigh and calf lengthen while the ankle holds a turned-out position.
  • The muscles around the shoulder blades work continuously to hold the arms at horizontal, which is why a minute feels long.

Modifications

Each of these keeps the wide stance and the horizontal reach. They differ only in how much load the legs carry.

Seated in a chair

Sit sideways on a sturdy chair with the right thigh along the seat and the right knee bent over the front edge. Extend the left leg out to the side with the foot flat and turned slightly in. Open both arms to shoulder height and turn the head over the right hand. You get the full upper-body shape and the back-leg line with no standing load at all. This is a complete version of the pose, not a preparation for one.

Back against a wall

Set up with your back, or the back of the shoulders and hips, lightly touching a wall. The wall tells you immediately when the torso starts leaning toward the front leg, which is otherwise very hard to feel. Useful for anyone whose balance is uncertain in a wide stance.

Chair under the front thigh

Place a chair so the front thigh rests lightly on the seat when the knee bends. The chair carries some of the weight so the front thigh does less work, letting you hold the shape long enough to actually study the alignment.

Shorter stance, less bend

Narrow the stance by a foot and bend the front knee only to forty-five degrees. Everything else stays identical. This is the version to use on a tired day, in the first weeks, or when you want to hold it for two full minutes.

Hands on the hips

If holding the arms out is the limiting factor, rest the hands on the hips. It removes the shoulder endurance demand entirely and lets you concentrate on what the legs and pelvis are doing.

Blanket under the back foot

If the back heel will not settle to the floor, place a folded blanket or a low block beneath it. Grounding the back foot changes the whole shape, and it is worth propping to get it.

Progression

  • Deeper front knee. Work toward a ninety degree bend over several weeks, without letting the knee drift inward or the torso lean.
  • Longer holds. Build from 30 seconds toward 90 seconds a side. Leg endurance improves faster than most people expect.
  • Extended Side Angle, Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana. From the same base, lower the front forearm onto the front thigh and reach the top arm past the ear.
  • Reverse Warrior. Turn the front palm up and reach the front arm overhead, letting the back hand slide down the back leg. Keep the front knee bent as it is.

This pose appears in the 10-Minute Morning sequence and again in the Gentle Full Practice.

Contraindications and care

Approach with care or use the chair version if: you have knee pain, a history of knee injury or a knee replacement, hip pain in a turned-out position, an ankle that rolls easily, or difficulty holding a standing position for a minute at a time. If a shoulder does not comfortably reach horizontal, keep the hands on the hips.

Skip it and speak with a professional first if: you are recovering from recent surgery to the knee, hip, ankle or spine, or you have been advised to avoid loaded, turned-out leg positions.

Never let a bent knee travel inward under load. If you cannot keep the knee tracking outward, shorten the stance or use the chair version. Work with a qualified teacher in person for hands-on guidance.
