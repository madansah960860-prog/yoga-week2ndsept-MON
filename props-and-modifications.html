Props and modifications

A prop does one of three jobs: it brings the floor closer, it extends your reach, or it holds weight so you do not have to. That is the whole theory. Everything below is which object does which job, and when.

Props are not training wheels. In several traditions they are standard equipment for everyone in the room regardless of experience, and a supported version of a pose is frequently a better use of five minutes than an unsupported one you are fighting.

If you buy in this order, nothing is wasted

  1. Two blocks. The most useful purchase by a distance. Used in standing folds, lunges, seated shapes and supported back bends.
  2. One strap. Cheap, small, and it makes seated and reclining leg work available to almost everyone.
  3. A mat, if your floor is hard, cold or slippery. On carpet you can genuinely wait.
  4. A firm blanket. Sits under the hips, under the knees, under the head. A folded wool camping blanket is ideal.
  5. A bolster, last, and only if you find you like long supported holds. Two firm pillows do the job until then.

Blocks

The job: bringing the floor closer, and giving something to press against.

A block has three heights depending on which face it sits on, usually about 4 inches, 6 inches and 9 inches. Choosing the height is the skill. In a standing forward fold, the tallest face under each hand lets you keep a long spine that would otherwise round. In a lunge, blocks either side of the front foot stop you having to reach the floor at all.

Material. Foam is light, forgiving and fine for almost everything. Cork is heavier, firmer and more stable when you stand or lean on it, and it does not compress. Wood is the firmest and least forgiving. For a first pair, foam.

Buy two, not one. A single block is only useful in a handful of shapes. Two blocks means you can support both hands, both knees, or a hand and a hip.

Household substitute. A stack of thick hardback books taped together, or a sturdy shoebox filled with something solid. Both work for hands. Neither is safe to stand on.

Used in: Downward-Facing Dog, Bridge Pose, standing folds, lunges, seated forward folds, supported bound angle.

Straps

The job: extending your reach so you can hold something without hauling on it.

A strap is a flat woven belt, usually 6 or 8 feet long, with a buckle. Loop it around a foot and you can work with a leg at length without rounding your spine to grab your toes. Loop it around both upper arms and it stops the elbows splaying in arm-supported shapes.

Length. Six feet is enough for most uses and most heights. Eight feet is worth it if you are tall or want to make loops around the body.

Buckle type. A D-ring buckle is easy to adjust one-handed. A plastic cinch buckle holds well but is fiddlier to release under tension.

Household substitute. A bathrobe belt, a long scarf, a dog leash or a luggage strap. All fine. Avoid anything stretchy, since the whole point is that it does not give.

Used in: reclining leg stretches, seated forward folds, shoulder work, and around the thighs in Bridge Pose.

Blankets

The job: adding height under the hips, padding under joints, and warmth when you stop moving.

The single most common seated modification in all of yoga is sitting on a folded blanket. Raising the hips two to four inches tilts the pelvis forward slightly, which lets the lower back keep its natural curve instead of rounding backward. If cross-legged sitting is uncomfortable, this fixes it more often than anything else.

What to look for. Firm and dense rather than fluffy. A traditional Mexican-style cotton yoga blanket folds into a stable brick. A fleece throw compresses to nothing and is not much use as support, though it is good as a cover.

Household substitute. A folded bath towel, a firm cushion, or a couch cushion turned on its side.

Used for: under the hips in any seated shape, under the knees in kneeling shapes, under the ankles in Child's Pose, under the shoulders in Bridge, and over you in the final rest.

Bolsters

The job: holding the weight of your torso so nothing has to work.

A bolster is a firm stuffed cylinder or rectangle, usually about 25 inches long. It is the defining prop of restorative practice, because it is the thing that lets a shape be held for ten minutes without effort. Rectangular bolsters are more stable and stack better. Round ones give a higher, more domed support.

Firmness matters more than size. A soft bolster compresses under your weight and stops supporting you, which defeats the purpose.

Household substitute. Two firm bed pillows stacked and pressed into a line, a rolled duvet, or a couch cushion with a folded blanket on top. These work genuinely well and there is no reason to buy a bolster until you know you want one.

Used in: supported Child's Pose, reclining bound angle, under the knees in Corpse Pose, and throughout the Evening Wind-Down sequence.

The chair

The job: raising the whole practice to seat height.

A chair is the most under-rated prop in yoga. It is not a lesser option for people who cannot get to the floor; it is a stable, height-adjustable surface that makes a large part of the practice available in a completely different way.

Which chair. No wheels, no swivel, a flat firm seat, and either no arms or arms low enough to stay out of the way. A dining chair. Put it against a wall for anything where you press into it.

Three things a chair does that nothing else does. It gives you a waist-height surface for the hands in standing shapes. It supports the thigh in a standing lunge so the leg does not have to hold the load. And it lets you build an entire practice sitting down, which our Desk Reset sequence does in eight minutes.

Chair versions in the library. Roughly two thirds of the poses on this site have a chair version described in full, including Warrior Two, Tree Pose, Mountain Pose and Downward-Facing Dog.

The wall

The job: giving you a straight, immovable reference.

Free, already installed, and the most honest feedback device in the room. A wall tells you where vertical actually is, which is information your own sense of position is surprisingly bad at supplying.

Behind you. Stand with your heels a couple of inches out and let the pelvis, shoulder blades and head make contact. You immediately learn where your spine sits.

Beside you. One hand on the wall turns any balance into a shape you can study rather than survive.

In front of you. Hands on the wall gives you a version of Downward-Facing Dog with almost no wrist load.

Underneath you. Legs Up the Wall needs nothing but four feet of clear floor and a wall to rest against.

Clear the space first. Move the furniture and check what is hanging above you before you swing your legs up.

The mat

The job: grip, and a small amount of padding.

A mat's main function is stopping your hands and feet sliding on a smooth floor. Padding is secondary, and most mats provide less of it than people expect.

Thickness. Around 4 to 5 millimeters, roughly 3/16 of an inch, is the standard and suits most practice. Thicker mats around 6 millimeters are kinder to knees but less stable in standing balances. Thin travel mats around 2 millimeters give the best grip and almost no cushioning.

Length. Standard mats are 68 inches long, roughly 5 feet 8 inches. If you are taller than that, a 72 inch mat is worth the small extra cost.

Material. PVC is durable and cheap but not biodegradable. TPE is lighter and recyclable. Natural rubber grips best and is heaviest, and has a strong smell for the first few weeks. Cork over rubber grips better as you get warmer.

Household substitute. Carpet, a folded blanket on a rug, or a large towel on a non-slip surface. A towel on a hardwood floor is not a substitute, it is a hazard.

Building a whole practice at chair height

If floor practice is not available to you, here is the structure. It is the same arc as any other practice, at a different altitude.

  • Arrive. Seated Mountain, two minutes of even breathing.
  • Move the spine. Seated Cat and Cow for spinal flexion and extension, then a side bend each way, then a twist each way. That is four of the spine's directions covered in five minutes.
  • Shoulders. Eagle arms, then arms overhead, then hands clasped behind the back if that is available.
  • Hips. Figure four on each side, then a wide-knee forward fold over the thighs.
  • Standing shapes at the chair. Hands on the chair back for a standing fold, a lunge with the front foot forward, or Warrior Two sitting sideways on the seat.
  • Rest. Fold forward with the forearms on the thighs, or rest the forehead on a stack of cushions on a table.

How to decide you need a prop

Three questions, asked in the shape.

  • Can you breathe evenly? If not, you need support or less range.
  • Is your spine doing what the pose intends? If a fold has become a round back, you need height under the hands or the hips.
  • Could you stay here for the suggested hold? If the answer is obviously no at ten seconds, prop it and stay for the whole time instead.

Answering yes to all three without a prop does not mean you should not use one. Some shapes are simply more informative supported.

Using props safely

Do not stand on foam blocks. Put a chair against a wall for anything you press into. Check that a bolster or cushion stack cannot slide before you put weight on it. Clear the floor and the wall before Legs Up the Wall.
