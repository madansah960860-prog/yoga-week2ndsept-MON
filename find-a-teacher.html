Find a teacher

A website can describe a shape. It cannot see you standing in it. Practicing at home and seeing a qualified teacher in person occasionally is a considerably better combination than either on its own, and a single class can correct something you would have carried for years.

This page covers what to look for, what to ask before you book, and a directory of listings organized by state.

What to look for

  • They ask before class, not after. A teacher who wants to know about injuries, pregnancy, surgery or anything you have been told to avoid, before the class starts, is telling you something useful about how they work.
  • They offer options without ceremony. Good teaching sounds like here is the shape, here is the same shape with a block, here is the same shape in a chair, said in one breath and with equal weight.
  • They ask before touching. Consent for hands-on adjustment should be requested explicitly, and declining should cost you nothing. Many studios now use a consent card system. A teacher who adjusts you without asking is a reason to try somewhere else.
  • They watch the room. A teacher who demonstrates every pose from the front of the room for the whole class is not looking at anybody.
  • They do not diagnose. A yoga teacher is not a physician or a physical therapist unless they separately hold that license. If a teacher offers to fix a medical condition, that is a signal to leave.

Questions worth asking before booking

  • Is this class suitable for someone who has not practiced before?
  • Are props available, and are they used in class as a matter of course?
  • How many people are usually in the room?
  • Do you offer hands-on adjustments, and how do you ask about them?
  • Can I arrive ten minutes early to talk about a limitation I have?
  • What is the room temperature?
  • Is there anything I need to bring?

A studio that answers these clearly and without irritation is usually a studio worth trying. One that does not, is telling you something too.

A note about credentials

Yoga teaching is not licensed by any US state. The most commonly cited credential is registration with a national yoga alliance organization at the 200-hour or 500-hour level, which records that a teacher completed a training program of a certain length at a registered school. It is a record of training hours. It is not a license, not a clinical qualification, and not a guarantee of teaching quality.

Some teachers hold additional, genuinely separate qualifications: physical therapy, athletic training, nursing, medicine. Those are licensed professions with their own regulators, and a teacher holding one should be able to tell you which license, which state and which number.

Every credential shown in the directory below is supplied by the teacher and is marked as unverified by us. We do not check credentials and we do not endorse anyone listed. Ask directly, and ask for specifics.

List yourself, free

Any yoga teacher or studio in the United States can have a basic listing at no cost. A free listing includes your name or business name, city and state, the styles you teach, a short description in your own words, and one link.

What we ask in return: that the description is accurate, that it makes no medical or therapeutic claims, and that any credential you state is real and specific enough to be checked by the person reading it.

A note on responsibility

First Light Yoga does not verify credentials, insurance, background checks or teaching quality for anyone in this directory. A listing is not a recommendation, an endorsement or a warranty. Do your own checking, ask direct questions, and trust your own impression of a first class.
