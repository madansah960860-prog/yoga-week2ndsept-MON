Practice at home

You do not need a room. You need about 24 square feet of floor, a wall you can reach, and somewhere to keep two or three objects so that starting takes fifteen seconds rather than five minutes.

Most home practices fail on logistics rather than motivation. If the mat lives at the back of a closet behind the vacuum, the practice happens on the days you were going to do it anyway. This page is about removing those small obstacles.

How much space

A standard mat is 68 inches by 24 inches, roughly 5 feet 8 inches by 2 feet. What you actually need is that plus room to take your arms out to the sides and to step your feet wide, which comes to about 8 feet by 4 feet, or 32 square feet.

The practical test: lie on your back with your arms out in a T and check that nothing is within a hand's width of your fingertips. Then stand and take a wide stance, arms out at shoulder height, and check the same. If you pass both, the space is enough for every sequence on this site.

If you only have less. A 6 feet by 3 feet area still covers everything except the widest standing shapes. Wide-Legged Forward Fold and Warrior Two can both be done with a shorter stance. Practicing in a small space is a limitation, not a disqualification.

The wall is part of the setup

Pick a spot where at least one wall is clear from the floor up to about 4 feet, with nothing hanging on it and nothing leaning against it. That gives you the wall version of Downward-Facing Dog, wall support for balances, back-to-wall alignment checks, and Legs Up the Wall. Losing the wall costs you more useful options than losing a couple of feet of floor.

The surface underneath

Hardwood or tile. Hard and slippery. You want a mat, and you want to check it does not slide on the surface. A thin rug under the mat solves sliding.

Low-pile carpet. The easiest surface to start on. Enough grip for bare feet, enough padding for knees, and you can practice for weeks without a mat. Standing balances are slightly harder on carpet because the surface gives.

Thick or shag carpet. Padding is good, stability is poor. A mat on top helps, though it will still feel soft in standing shapes.

Concrete, garage or basement. Cold and unforgiving. A thicker mat, around 6 millimeters, plus a blanket under the knees.

Outdoors. Grass is uneven but pleasant. A deck is usually fine. Check for anything sharp before you put your hands down, and be aware that direct sun on a mat makes it hot enough to be uncomfortable within minutes.

Light and temperature

Light. Whatever you have. Facing a window in the morning is pleasant; being blinded by it in a standing shape is not, so set up at ninety degrees to it rather than directly toward it. For evening practice, a lamp is better than an overhead light, since you spend part of the practice looking at the ceiling.

Temperature. Room temperature is fine, meaning roughly 68 to 72 degrees Fahrenheit. Two practical notes: you will feel colder in the last five minutes than in the first five, because you stop moving, so have a blanket or a sweater within reach. And a cold floor in winter is a genuine reason a practice stops happening; a rug fixes it.

Air. Open a window if the room is stuffy. Nothing on this site requires a heated room, and heating a room at home to studio hot-yoga temperatures is not something to attempt casually.

Storage that keeps the habit

The rule: everything you need lives within arm's reach of where you practice, and getting set up takes under thirty seconds.

  • Mat. Rolled and standing upright in a corner, or flat under a sofa. Not in a cupboard behind other things.
  • Blocks and strap. In a basket by the wall. A shallow box under a bed also works if the bed is in the practice room.
  • Blanket. Folded over the arm of a chair, where it is also useful for sitting.
  • Bolster. Large and awkward. If you have one, it lives out, usually as a cushion on a bed or sofa.

If your practice space is also the living room, keeping a mat permanently rolled out is unrealistic. Aim instead for the thirty-second setup: mat out, props from the basket, done.

Screens, sound and interruptions

Screens. If you use the sequence timer on this site, set the phone or tablet on the floor at the front of your mat where you can glance at it without turning your head sharply. Put the device on do not disturb first. A notification arriving mid-practice ends more sessions than fatigue does.

Sound. Optional. Instrumental music at low volume is common. What matters more is that you can hear your own breath, which is the main feedback you have when practicing alone.

Other people. A closed door helps if you have one. If you do not, practicing in a shared space is entirely normal and gets easier once the people you live with stop finding it interesting, which takes about a week.

Pets. There is no solution. A cat will sit on the mat. Accept it or shut the door.

Time of day

Morning. Bodies are generally stiffer earlier, so expect less range and start slower. The advantage is that nothing has had a chance to get in the way yet, which is why the 10-Minute Morning sequence starts on the floor rather than standing.

Midday. Usually the most available range of movement and the hardest time to protect in a schedule. The Desk Reset exists for exactly this slot.

Evening. Convenient and easy to keep. Long supported holds suit the end of a day, which is what the Evening Wind-Down is built from.

The best time is the one that repeats. Consistency at a mediocre hour beats perfection at an hour you cannot defend.

A four-week starting plan

Fixed, unambitious, and designed so that missing a day does not break it.

  • Week 1. The 10-Minute Morning, three times. Same sequence each time. Read Start Here before the first one.
  • Week 2. The same sequence, three times, plus one Desk Reset on a day you are sitting a lot.
  • Week 3. Three mornings, plus one Evening Wind-Down. This is the week the shapes start to feel like yours.
  • Week 4. Two mornings, one evening, and one attempt at the Gentle Full Practice on a day you have half an hour.

If you miss a day, do the next one. There is nothing to make up.

Practicing in small or shared spaces

A studio apartment. Practice beside the bed and use it. The edge of a bed is a good height for hands in a standing fold, and a mattress makes an acceptable surface for the supported shapes in the Evening Wind-Down.

A hotel room. Move the chair, use the floor at the foot of the bed, and use the wall by the door. The Desk Reset needs only a chair and works anywhere.

An office. The chair sequence is designed to be done without changing clothes and without lying on the floor of a workplace.

Upstairs with someone below. Nothing in the sequences on this site involves jumping. Weight shifts in standing shapes are quiet.

Practicing with limited mobility

If getting down to and up from the floor is difficult or unsafe, build the practice at chair height. The chair section of the props guide describes the full structure, and roughly two thirds of the pose library has a seated or standing version written out in the same detail as the floor version. That is a complete practice, not a compromise.

Practicing safely at home

Practicing alone means nobody is watching you, so build in your own margins. Clear the space before you start, check the floor for anything that slides, and stay within a range where you could stop at any moment.
