Styles explained

Class listings tend to name a style and assume you know what it means. Here are six of the most common names in the United States, described by what actually happens in the room: how fast it moves, how long you stay, how many props are on the floor and how much is spoken out loud.

None of these is a beginner style or an advanced style. Every one of them can be taught well for people who have never practiced, and every one of them can be taught in a way that leaves a beginner behind. The teacher matters more than the label.

Hatha

The general-purpose label. Slow enough to hear the instruction, long enough to feel the shape.

Strictly, hatha names the whole physical branch of yoga, which makes every style on this page a hatha style. In an American studio schedule it means something narrower: a class that holds poses for around thirty seconds to a minute, moves between them without a set flowing pattern, and spends time on how to enter each shape.

What a class looks like. Sixty minutes. Some seated breathing at the start, a warm-up on the floor, a standing section, some floor work, and a rest at the end. The teacher talks throughout. Props are available and often used, but the class does not revolve around them.

Who tends to like it. People who want to learn the shapes rather than be led through them quickly, and people returning after a long gap. It is the most common recommendation for a first class, and generally a good one.

What might surprise you. Hatha is not automatically easy. A minute in Warrior Two is a long minute.

Vinyasa

Continuous movement, each shape linked to an inhale or an exhale.

Vinyasa means placing in a special way, and in practice it means the poses are strung together so that one movement follows another with the breath setting the rhythm. You hold each shape for a few breaths at most. The class has an arc rather than a fixed list, and it varies from teacher to teacher and week to week.

What a class looks like. Sixty minutes, often warm. Repeated rounds of a linking sequence, usually some version of plank, lowering down, upward-facing shape, downward-facing dog. Standing sequences that build up one pose at a time. Music is common.

Who tends to like it. People who prefer to keep moving, and people who find long holds uncomfortable to sit with.

What might surprise you. The linking sequence puts a lot of repeated load through the wrists and shoulders. If you are new, it is completely normal to skip the linking movement and rest in Child's Pose while the room does it. Say so to the teacher before class and they will tell you the same thing.

Yin

Few poses, held for several minutes each, mostly seated or on the floor.

Yin classes hold a small number of shapes for two to five minutes, sometimes longer. Muscles are deliberately kept relaxed rather than engaged, and the poses are almost entirely seated or lying down. The intent is to spend time in a shape rather than to work in it.

What a class looks like. Sixty to seventy-five minutes covering perhaps eight to twelve shapes. Bolsters, blocks and blankets everywhere. Long quiet stretches with little instruction once you are in a pose. A timer or a chime marking each hold.

Who tends to like it. People who want a slower practice, and people who already do a lot of fast movement elsewhere.

What might surprise you. Long holds are mentally demanding. Three minutes with nothing to do but stay is harder for most people than three minutes of movement. Coming out of a long hold slowly matters, and a shape can feel fine at minute two and not at minute four, so keep checking.

Restorative

Fully supported shapes held for a long time, with nothing working.

Restorative practice props the body so completely that no muscular effort is needed to stay in a shape. Holds run from five to twenty minutes. There are usually only four to six poses in a whole class, and much of the class time is spent setting each one up.

What a class looks like. Sixty to ninety minutes, warm and often dimly lit. Two or three bolsters per person, several blankets, blocks, eye covers. The teacher moves around adjusting props. Long silences.

Who tends to like it. People who are tired, people recovering from a period of high activity, and people who find it hard to stop.

What might surprise you. How much setup it takes, and how strange it feels at first to be given permission to do nothing for twenty minutes. Our Evening Wind-Down sequence is built on this idea at a shorter scale.

Iyengar

Precise alignment, long instruction, props used as a matter of course.

Named after B. K. S. Iyengar, this method is defined by attention to alignment detail and by systematic prop use. Classes are structured, teachers are trained for a long time before certifying, and there is more explanation per pose than in almost any other style.

What a class looks like. Seventy-five to ninety minutes. Bright room, wall ropes sometimes, a wall of blocks and belts. Fewer poses, each taken apart and rebuilt. Demonstrations. Specific instructions about which part of which foot presses down.

Who tends to like it. People who want to understand exactly what they are doing, and people who need substantial modification and want a room where props are the norm rather than the exception.

What might surprise you. The volume of detail. It can feel like a lot in a first class, and it is worth going back a second time before deciding.

Ashtanga

A fixed sequence, practiced in the same order every time, at pace.

Ashtanga follows set series of poses in a fixed order. Most people practice the Primary Series, which takes around ninety minutes. Poses are held for five breaths and linked by a repeated movement pattern. The order does not change from day to day, which is the whole point.

What a class looks like. Either a led class where a teacher calls the sequence, or a Mysore-style room where everyone practices their own portion of the sequence at their own pace while the teacher moves between people. The Mysore room is quiet apart from breathing.

Who tends to like it. People who want a repeatable, measurable practice and a strong physical demand, and people who like knowing exactly what is coming.

What might surprise you. A Mysore room is beginner-friendly in a way that looks intimidating from the doorway. New students are usually taught a short opening portion and nothing more, which can take twenty minutes rather than ninety. Ask before you assume you need to keep up with the room.

Other words you will see on a schedule

  • Power yoga. Usually vinyasa with more strength work and less Sanskrit. Pace is brisk.
  • Hot yoga. Describes the room, not the style. Rooms typically run from 90 to 105 degrees Fahrenheit. Bring water and expect to need it.
  • Slow flow. Vinyasa with longer holds and fewer rounds of the linking sequence.
  • Chair yoga. A whole practice done seated or using a chair for support. Our Desk Reset is an eight-minute example.
  • Prenatal. Classes designed around pregnancy. Worth seeking out specifically rather than modifying a general class on your own.
  • Kundalini. A distinct tradition with repetitive movement, breath techniques and chanting. Quite different in feel from the six above.
  • Level 1, Level 2, All levels. Studio-specific and inconsistent between studios. Call and ask what they mean by it.

How to choose a first class

Pick by pace before you pick by name. If you want to be told how to do things and given time to do them, look for hatha, Iyengar or a slow flow. If you want to move, look for vinyasa. If you want to lie down with props, look for restorative or yin.

Then call the studio and ask two questions: is this class suitable for someone who has not practiced before, and are props available. The answers, and the way they are given, will tell you most of what you need to know.

A note before your first class

Tell the teacher before class about injuries, pregnancy, recent surgery or anything you have been advised to avoid. A good teacher will want to know and will give you alternatives.
