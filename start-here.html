Beginner's guide

Start here

If you have never done yoga, or you tried a class years ago and left feeling behind, this page is the whole beginning. It takes about eight minutes to read, and you can practice for the first time straight afterward without buying a single thing.

What yoga is on this site

Yoga is a broad tradition with philosophical, ethical and meditative parts. What most people in the United States meet first is the physical side: a set of body positions called āsana, practiced with attention on the breath, usually in a set order.

That physical practice is what First Light Yoga writes about. We describe what a pose does mechanically, which joints move and which muscles lengthen or work, and we leave claims about what it might do for a health condition out of it entirely. Movement is worth doing on its own terms.

You will meet Sanskrit names throughout. They are given with correct diacritical marks alongside the English, because the Sanskrit is often the only name a teacher uses out loud. The glossary holds the vocabulary in one place.

What you actually need

Less than the internet suggests. Here is the honest order in which things matter.

Enough to begin today

  • A patch of floor about 6 feet by 3 feet. Enough to lie down with your arms slightly out.
  • A wall with roughly 4 feet of clear space. A wall is the most useful free prop there is.
  • A sturdy chair without arms, or with arms low enough to be out of the way. A dining chair is ideal, a wheeled office chair is not.
  • Clothes you can move and breathe in. Bare feet or grippy socks.

Worth adding later

  • A mat gives grip on a smooth floor and a bit of padding. On carpet you can go without one for a while.
  • Two foam blocks bring the floor closer in standing and seated shapes. The single most useful purchase.
  • A strap lengthens your reach so you can hold a foot without hauling on it. A bathrobe belt or a scarf works.
  • A firm blanket to sit on, to cushion knees, or to roll under an ankle.
  • A bolster for supported resting shapes. Two firm bed pillows in a stack do the same job.

Your first ten minutes

Six shapes in a fixed order. Read each linked guide once before you start, then run through the order below. Nothing here requires you to reach the floor with straight legs.

  1. Child's Pose, 1 minute. Start folded and low. Breathe into the back of the ribs and let the practice begin from a quiet place rather than an ambitious one.
  2. Cat and Cow, 8 slow rounds. On hands and knees, alternate between rounding the spine on the exhale and letting the belly drop on the inhale. This is the warm-up for every other spinal movement.
  3. Downward-Facing Dog, 30 to 45 seconds. Keep the knees bent. Bent knees in this shape are not a compromise, they are the version most people should be doing.
  4. Mountain Pose, 1 minute. Stand and notice how the two sides of your body differ. This is the reference position for every standing shape.
  5. Warrior Two, 45 seconds each side. A wide stance, a bent front knee, arms long. Use a chair under the front thigh if the legs shake.
  6. Bridge Pose, 3 rounds of 30 seconds, then rest. Finish on your back with the knees bent and simply breathe for a minute.

How to read a pose page

Every pose guide on this site is built from the same nine parts, in the same order, so you always know where to look.

  • Names. English first, then Sanskrit with diacritics and a rough pronunciation.
  • Difficulty. Beginner, intermediate or advanced, judged by coordination and load rather than by flexibility.
  • Entry steps. Numbered, in the order you actually move.
  • Alignment cues. What to check once you are in the shape.
  • Breath pattern. Where the inhale and exhale go, and whether the breath is used to move or to stay.
  • Hold time. A suggested range, not a target to beat.
  • Common mistakes. What usually goes wrong and the fix.
  • Modifications. At least two, using a chair, a wall, blocks or a strap. Written as equal options.
  • Progression and contraindications. Where the shape goes next, and who should approach it with care or skip it.

Breathing, without the mystique

For your first month, one instruction covers it: breathe in and out through the nose, at a pace that stays comfortable, and do not hold your breath when a shape gets demanding.

Holding the breath is the most reliable sign that you have gone past a useful edge. If you cannot keep a steady nasal breath, back off the shape until you can. That is the whole rule.

Once that is habitual, the breathwork page covers four patterns in detail, with an on-screen pacer you can breathe along with, and clear notes on when to stop a pattern.

How often, and for how long

Short and frequent beats long and rare. Ten minutes on five days does more for your familiarity with the shapes than one ninety-minute session on a Sunday, and it is far easier to protect in a real week.

A sensible first month: three sessions a week of ten to fifteen minutes. Keep the same sequence for the whole month. Repetition is what turns a set of instructions into something your body recognizes, and the second week of the same sequence teaches you more than the first week of a new one.

After a month, add one longer session, or start swapping in new poses one at a time from the library. There is no schedule you are behind on.

Before you practice

This site provides general movement education. It is not medical advice and it does not replace care from a qualified healthcare professional. Talk with one before starting a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, recovering from surgery, or managing a health condition.

Stop if you feel pain. Sharp, pinching or radiating sensation is a signal to come out, not to push through. Work with a qualified teacher in person for hands-on guidance.
