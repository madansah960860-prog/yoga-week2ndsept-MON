Frequently asked questions

Grouped into four sections: starting out, practicing safely, using the site, and how the site is funded. If your question is not here, send it to us and we will answer it and probably add it.

Starting out

Yes, and this is the most common reason people put off starting. Flexibility is an outcome some people get from practicing, not a requirement for beginning. Every pose on this site has options that bring the floor up toward you using blocks, a chair or a wall. A standing forward fold with your hands on two blocks is the same pose as one with your hands on the floor.

Where flexibility genuinely varies between people, much of it is proportion and joint structure rather than effort. Whether your heels reach the floor in Downward-Facing Dog is largely a matter of ankle range and limb length, and it is not a measure of progress.

Then practice standing and seated in a chair. Roughly two thirds of the pose library has a chair or standing version written out in the same detail as the floor version, and the Desk Reset sequence is eight minutes done entirely in a chair, covering spinal flexion, extension, side bending and rotation both ways, plus both hips and the shoulders.

The chair section of the props guide sets out how to build a whole practice at seat height. It is a complete practice, not a reduced one.

Three sessions of ten to fifteen minutes a week is a realistic and effective first month. Keep the same sequence for the whole month. Repetition is what turns a set of written instructions into something your body recognizes, and the second week of the same sequence teaches you more than the first week of a new one.

Ten honest minutes five times beats ninety minutes once, and it is far easier to protect in a real week. Our guide on building a practice that survives a busy month goes into this properly.

No. A patch of floor about 6 feet by 3 feet, a clear wall and a sturdy chair cover the first month completely. On carpet you do not need a mat.

If you buy one thing later, buy two foam blocks. They change more poses more usefully than a mat does. The props guide lists a household substitute for every prop, because buying is genuinely optional.

For a first in-person class, look for hatha, a slow flow, or an Iyengar class. All three move at a pace that lets you hear the instruction. If you want to move continuously, vinyasa. If you want to lie down with props, restorative or yin.

The styles page compares six named styles by pace, hold length, prop use and room temperature. None of them is a beginner style or an advanced style; the teacher matters more than the label.

No. Inversions like headstand and shoulderstand are not a required destination and are not on this site. Plenty of experienced practitioners spend most of their time in shapes labeled beginner, which is exactly what those shapes are for. There is no ladder you are behind on.

Practicing safely

No. Broad muscular sensation spread across an area is expected and normal. Sharp, pinching, burning or radiating sensation, particularly in a joint or down a limb, is a signal to come out of the shape rather than to work through it.

The three checks that cover most situations: can you breathe evenly, is the sensation spread out rather than sharp in one point, and could you come out of the shape under control. If any answer is no, ease off.

That is a question for a qualified healthcare professional who knows your history, not for a website. We cannot assess an injury, tell you whether a specific pose is safe for you, or give medical advice, and we will say so if you email to ask.

What we can do is give you accurate information to take to that conversation. Every pose page carries a contraindication note listing who should approach it with care, so you can show it to a professional and ask directly.

Speak with the professional managing your pregnancy first. If they say yes, look for a dedicated prenatal class rather than modifying a general class on your own, because prenatal classes are designed around pregnancy from the start rather than adapted at the edges.

Several pages on this site carry notes about pregnancy where a shape is commonly discussed, for example that extended time lying flat on the back is generally discouraged in the second and third trimesters. Those notes are general information, not guidance for your pregnancy.

Reading a shape carefully, trying it slowly and adjusting is a reasonable way to learn a beginner practice, and it builds a durable understanding. It is not a substitute for a teacher watching you.

The honest recommendation is a combination: practice at home from written guides, and see a qualified teacher in person occasionally. A single class can correct something you would otherwise carry for years. Our directory is free to use.

We do not make claims of that kind, anywhere on this site. This is general movement education, not healthcare, and we describe what a pose does physically, which joints move and which muscles lengthen or work, rather than what it might do for a condition.

If you have a specific concern about your back, your sleep or anything else, a qualified healthcare professional is the right place to take it. They may well recommend movement, and if they do, they can tell you which kind.

No, and we do not intend to. No calorie figures, no fat-burning language, no before-and-after content, no body-shape commentary, and no diet, cleanse, fasting or detox material of any kind. If you find something on this site that reads that way, tell us and we will remove it.

Using the site

Every sequence page has a player. Press start and it counts down the first hold, then announces the next pose and moves on by itself. You can pause, skip forward or back, or click any pose in the list to jump straight to it. The list underneath is the sequence in plain text and reads perfectly well without the timer.

Yes. Every sequence page has a print button that produces a clean one-page practice sheet with the pose names, Sanskrit and hold times, without the navigation, ads or controls. Printing from your browser directly does the same thing. It is free and requires no account.

Because a full guide is a substantial piece of work. Each one carries names with diacritics, difficulty, entry steps, alignment cues, breath pattern, hold time, common mistakes, at least two modifications, a progression and a contraindication note, and none of that publishes until all of it is written and checked.

The other twenty-six entries in the library carry their names, difficulty, the body areas involved, the props that help and a description of the shape, which is enough to place them in a practice.

No. There is no account, no login and no email wall on any instructional page. The library, the sequences, the guides and every reference page are free to read as they are.

It is built to meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA, with semantic landmarks, a skip link, keyboard navigation, visible focus states, alt text on every image and motion that respects your operating system's reduced motion setting. If something does not work for you, please tell us. We treat accessibility reports as bugs. See the accessibility statement.

The standing note

Everything on this site is general movement education. It is not medical advice and it makes no claim to treat, prevent or improve any condition. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before beginning a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, recovering from surgery or managing a health condition. Stop if you feel pain, and work with a qualified teacher in person for hands-on guidance.
