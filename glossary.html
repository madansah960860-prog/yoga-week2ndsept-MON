Glossary

Ninety-seven terms you will meet in a class, on a schedule or in a book, with a rough pronunciation and a plain definition. Search by any part of a word, in English or Sanskrit, with or without the diacritical marks.

A note on the marks. Sanskrit is written here in the standard transliteration used in scholarly and yoga texts, so ā is a long a, ś and ṣ are two different sh sounds, and ṃ and ṅ are nasal sounds. The pronunciations given are approximations for an American English speaker, not exact.

Showing 97 of 97 terms

Adho

AH-doh

Downward. Appears in Adho Mukha Śvānāsana, downward-facing dog.

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana

AH-doh MOO-kah shvah-NAH-suh-nuh

Downward-facing dog. An inverted V on the hands and feet. Full guide.

Ahiṃsā

ah-HIM-sah

Non-harming. The first of the yamas, the ethical guidelines in Patañjali's eight-limbed system.

Añjali Mudrā

AHN-jah-lee MOO-drah

Palms pressed together at the chest. The gesture most people call prayer hands.

Añjaneyāsana

AHN-jah-nay-AH-suh-nuh

Low lunge. One foot forward, the back knee resting on the floor.

Ardha

ARD-hah

Half. Ardha Uttānāsana is the halfway lift of a standing forward fold.

Ardha Candrāsana

ARD-hah chan-DRAH-suh-nuh

Half moon. A standing balance with one leg lifted parallel to the floor and the torso turned open.

Ardha Matsyendrāsana

ARD-hah mot-see-en-DRAH-suh-nuh

Half lord of the fishes pose, the common seated twist. Named after the sage Matsyendra.

Āsana

AH-suh-nuh

Seat, and by extension a body position. The third limb of the eight-limbed system and the part most Americans meet first.

Aṣṭāṅga

ASH-tahn-gah

Eight-limbed. Refers both to Patañjali's eight limbs of yoga and to the modern method with fixed sequences. See styles explained.

Baddha

BAH-dah

Bound. Indicates a shape where the hands clasp or a limb is wrapped.

Baddha Koṇāsana

BAH-dah koh-NAH-suh-nuh

Bound angle pose. Seated with the soles of the feet together and the knees dropping wide.

Bālāsana

bah-LAH-suh-nuh

Child's pose. A kneeling forward rest. Full guide.

Bandha

BAHN-dah

Lock or bind. Refers to specific internal muscular engagements taught mainly in Ashtanga and traditional hatha.

Bhujaṅgāsana

boo-jahn-GAH-suh-nuh

Cobra pose. Lying face down and lifting the chest with the hands lightly loaded.

Bitilāsana

bee-tee-LAH-suh-nuh

Cow pose. The arching half of the cat and cow pair.

Cakra

CHUCK-ruh, often written chakra

Wheel. In some traditions, a set of energy centers described along the midline of the body. A concept from yogic and tantric texts rather than an anatomical structure.

Caturaṅga Daṇḍāsana

chah-tur-AHN-gah dahn-DAH-suh-nuh

Four-limbed staff pose. The low push-up position in a vinyasa linking sequence.

Cue

English, studio shorthand

A short instruction given while you are in a shape. Alignment cues describe what to check, breath cues describe when to inhale or exhale.

Daṇḍa

DAHN-dah

Staff or stick. Signals a straight, rigid line through the body, as in Daṇḍāsana, staff pose.

Dhāraṇā

dhah-rah-NAH

Concentration. The sixth of the eight limbs, holding attention on one thing.

Dhyāna

DHYAH-nah

Meditation. The seventh limb, sustained attention without effort.

Dṛṣṭi

DRISH-tee

Gaze point. A designated place to look in a pose. Fixing the gaze makes balancing noticeably easier.

Edge

English, studio shorthand

The point in a shape where sensation becomes noticeable but the breath stays even. Working at the edge means staying just short of strain.

Eka Pāda

EH-kah PAH-dah

One foot, or one leg. Signals a single-leg variation of a shape.

Flow

English, studio shorthand

A class or section where poses are linked continuously with the breath rather than held separately.

Garuḍāsana

gah-roo-DAH-suh-nuh

Eagle pose. Arms and legs wrapped across the midline while balancing on one foot.

Guru

GOO-roo

Teacher, in the traditional sense of one who transmits a lineage. In American studios the word teacher is used far more often.

Haṭha

HUT-hah

Force, or the sun and moon. Names the whole physical branch of yoga, and on a class schedule usually means a slower paced class with held poses.

Hip hinge

English, movement term

Folding forward by rotating the pelvis over the thigh bones while the spine stays long, as opposed to rounding forward from the middle of the back.

Jānu Śīrṣāsana

JAH-noo sheer-SHAH-suh-nuh

Head-to-knee pose. A seated fold over one straight leg with the other foot drawn to the inner thigh.

Jaṭhara Parivartānāsana

JUT-hah-rah pah-ree-var-tah-NAH-suh-nuh

Revolved abdomen pose. A supine twist with the legs lowering to one side.

Koṇa

KOH-nah

Angle. Appears in Baddha Koṇāsana and Upaviṣṭha Koṇāsana.

Kumbhaka

KOOM-bah-kah

Breath retention, holding the breath in or out. Not suitable for everyone. See the safety notes on the breathwork page.

Mālāsana

mah-LAH-suh-nuh

Garland pose, a deep squat with the feet close together and the heels working toward the floor.

Mantra

MAHN-truh

A sound, word or phrase repeated as a focus for attention.

Mārjārīāsana

mar-jar-ee-AH-suh-nuh

Cat pose. The rounding half of the cat and cow pair.

Mudrā

MOO-drah

Seal or gesture, usually of the hands. Añjali mudrā is the most common one in a class.

Mukha

MOO-kah

Face, or facing. Adho Mukha is downward-facing, Ūrdhva Mukha is upward-facing.

Mūla Bandha

MOO-lah BAHN-dah

Root lock. A specific pelvic floor engagement taught in some traditions. Best learned in person rather than from text.

Nāḍī

NAH-dee

Channel. In yogic texts, a pathway along which prāṇa is described as moving.

Nāḍī Śodhana

NAH-dee SHOH-dah-nah

Alternate nostril breathing, in which one nostril is closed at a time with the fingers.

Namas te

NAH-mah-stay, usually written namaste

A greeting in several South Asian languages, meaning roughly a bowing to you. Used at the end of many American classes, a convention that has no fixed traditional meaning in that setting.

Nāvāsana

nah-VAH-suh-nuh

Boat pose. Balancing on the sit bones with the legs lifted and the torso leaning back.

Neutral spine

English, movement term

The spine holding its three natural curves, inward at the lower back and neck and outward at the upper back, rather than being flattened or exaggerated.

Niyama

nee-YAH-mah

Observances. The second limb, a set of five personal practices including svādhyāya, self-study, and tapas, disciplined effort.

Auṃ

AH-oo-m, usually written om

A syllable chanted at the beginning or end of some classes. Optional in every class we know of, and not chanting it is unremarkable.

Pāda

PAH-dah

Foot or leg. Pādāṅguṣṭha means big toe.

Padmāsana

pod-MAH-suh-nuh

Lotus pose. A cross-legged seat with each foot on the opposite thigh. It asks a great deal of the hips and knees and is not a beginner shape.

Parivṛtta

pah-ree-VRIT-tah

Revolved. Signals a twisted version of a shape, as in revolved triangle.

Pārśva

PARSH-vah

Side, or flank. Appears in Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana, extended side angle.

Paścimottānāsana

POSH-chee-moh-tah-NAH-suh-nuh

Seated forward fold. Literally an intense stretch of the west, meaning the back of the body.

Phalakāsana

fah-lah-KAH-suh-nuh

Plank pose. A straight line from head to heels supported on the hands and toes.

Prāṇa

PRAH-nah

Life force or vital energy in yogic thought, often associated with the breath. A traditional concept, not a physiological measurement.

Prāṇāyāma

prah-nah-YAH-mah

Breathing practice. The fourth limb. Four patterns explained.

Prasārita Pādottānāsana

prah-SAH-ree-tah pah-doh-tah-NAH-suh-nuh

Wide-legged standing forward fold.

Pratyāhāra

prah-tyah-HAH-rah

Withdrawal of the senses. The fifth limb, turning attention inward rather than outward.

Prop

English, studio shorthand

Any object used to support a pose: block, strap, blanket, bolster, chair, wall. Full guide.

Rāja

RAH-jah

King, or royal. Eka Pāda Rājakapotāsana is one-legged king pigeon pose.

Salamba

sah-LOM-bah

Supported. Salamba Bhujaṅgāsana, sphinx pose, is the forearm-supported cobra.

Śalabhāsana

shah-lah-BAH-suh-nuh

Locust pose. Lying face down and lifting the chest, arms and legs without pushing with the hands.

Sama Vṛtti

SAH-mah VRIT-tee

Even fluctuation. A breathing pattern with the inhale and exhale of equal length.

Samasthiti

sah-mah-STHEE-tee

Equal standing. Used in some traditions for the standing position others call Tāḍāsana.

Sarvāṅga

sar-VAHN-gah

All limbs, or whole body. Appears in Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana and Sālamba Sarvāṅgāsana, shoulderstand.

Śavāsana

shah-VAH-suh-nuh

Corpse pose. Lying flat and still at the end of a practice. The pose most often cut and most often missed.

Sequence

English, studio shorthand

A set of poses in a chosen order. Four complete sequences here.

Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana

SAY-too BAHN-duh sar-vahn-GAH-suh-nuh

Bridge pose. Lying on the back and lifting the hips. Full guide.

Siddhāsana

sid-DHAH-suh-nuh

Accomplished pose. A cross-legged seat with one heel drawn in close to the body.

Sit bones

English, anatomy shorthand

The two bony points at the base of the pelvis that carry your weight when you sit upright. Anatomically the ischial tuberosities.

Sukhāsana

soo-KAH-suh-nuh

Easy pose, a simple cross-legged seat. Considerably easier on a folded blanket than on the bare floor.

Supta

SOOP-tah

Reclining. Signals a version of a pose done lying on the back.

Supta Baddha Koṇāsana

SOOP-tah BAH-dah koh-NAH-suh-nuh

Reclining bound angle. On the back with the soles together and the knees supported wide.

Supta Pādāṅguṣṭhāsana

SOOP-tah pah-dahn-goosh-TAH-suh-nuh

Reclining hand-to-big-toe pose, usually practiced with a strap around the foot.

Sūrya Namaskāra

SOOR-yah nah-mahs-KAH-rah

Sun salutation. A short linked sequence used as a warm-up, with several standard variations.

Svādhyāya

svahd-HYAH-yah

Self-study. One of the niyamas, covering both study of texts and observation of your own patterns.

Tabletop

English, studio shorthand

The hands-and-knees position, with hands under the shoulders and knees under the hips. The starting point for cat and cow.

Tāḍāsana

tah-DAH-suh-nuh

Mountain pose. Standing, done deliberately. Full guide.

Tapas

TAH-pahs

Heat, in the sense of disciplined effort. One of the niyamas. Nothing to do with the Spanish plates.

Transition

English, studio shorthand

The movement between two poses. Transitions are where most awkwardness happens, and slowing them down is usually the fix.

Trikoṇāsana

tree-koh-NAH-suh-nuh

Triangle pose. A wide stance with both legs straight and the torso tipping sideways.

Ujjāyī

oo-JAH-yee

Victorious breath. A slight narrowing at the back of the throat that gives the breath a soft audible sound.

Upaviṣṭha Koṇāsana

oo-pah-VEESH-tah koh-NAH-suh-nuh

Wide-angle seated forward fold, with the legs open in a V.

Ūrdhva

OORD-vah

Upward. Ūrdhva Hastāsana is upward hands, the arms-overhead version of Mountain Pose.

Ūrdhva Mukha Śvānāsana

OORD-vah MOO-kah shvah-NAH-suh-nuh

Upward-facing dog. A prone back bend with the thighs lifted off the floor and the arms straight.

Utkaṭāsana

oot-kah-TAH-suh-nuh

Chair pose, sometimes translated as fierce pose. Knees bending as if sitting back onto a stool.

Uttānāsana

oo-tah-NAH-suh-nuh

Standing forward fold. Folding from the hips with the knees soft.

Utthita

oo-TEE-tah

Extended. Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana is extended side angle.

Viparīta Karaṇī

vee-pah-REE-tah kah-rah-NEE

Legs up the wall. A supported shape needing only a wall and clear floor.

Vinyāsa

vin-YAH-sah

Placing in a special way. Names both the linking of movement to breath and a style of class built on it.

Vīrabhadrāsana

veer-ah-bah-DRAH-suh-nuh

Warrior pose, named after the warrior Vīrabhadra. Numbered one, two and three. Warrior Two guide.

Vṛkṣāsana

vrik-SHAH-suh-nuh

Tree pose. A single-leg balance. Full guide.

Warm-up

English, studio shorthand

The opening section of a practice, usually slower and closer to the floor, that moves the joints through easy range before anything is loaded.

Yama

YAH-mah

Restraints. The first limb, a set of five ethical guidelines beginning with ahiṃsā, non-harming.

Yin

English, from Chinese

A modern style of long-held floor poses with the muscles relaxed. The name borrows from Chinese thought rather than from Sanskrit.

Yoga

YOH-gah

To yoke or to join. Names a family of Indian philosophical and practical traditions, of which the physical practice is one part.

Yoga Nidrā

YOH-gah NEE-drah

A guided practice done lying down, following spoken instructions that move attention around the body. Not a physical practice.

Yoga Sūtras

YOH-gah SOO-truhs

A short classical text attributed to Patañjali, setting out the eight limbs. It says almost nothing about physical postures.

No terms match that search. Try a shorter word, or search without the diacritical marks.

Reading Sanskrit pose names

Most pose names are compounds, and once you know a handful of parts you can decode names you have never seen. Utthita means extended, pārśva means side, koṇa means angle, and āsana means pose, so Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana is extended side angle pose. Supta means reclining and baddha means bound, so Supta Baddha Koṇāsana is reclining bound angle pose.

Direction words are similarly reusable. Adho is downward, ūrdhva is upward, mukha is facing, parivṛtta is revolved, ardha is half, eka pāda is one-legged. Names built from those cover a large part of a typical class.

Pronunciation varies between teachers and between traditions, and nobody expects precision from a student. Saying a name imperfectly is better than not learning it.
