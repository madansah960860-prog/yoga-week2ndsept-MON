Pose guide

Tree Pose

Vṛkṣāsana, pronounced vrik-SHAH-suh-nuh

  • Beginner
  • Balance
  • Wall or chair option
  • Hold 30 to 60 seconds each side

A single-leg balance with the lifted foot resting against the standing leg. Balance varies day to day and hour to hour for everyone, and a wobbly Tuesday says nothing about your practice. Standing near a wall is not cheating, it is how most people should learn this shape.

Entry steps

  1. Begin in Mountain Pose. Feet hip-width, weight even, arms by the sides. Take a couple of breaths here first.
  2. Choose a still point. Fix your eyes on something that does not move, at eye level, about six to eight feet away. Balance improves immediately with a steady gaze.
  3. Shift onto the left foot. Move the weight across, spreading the toes. Keep the standing knee soft rather than locked.
  4. Turn the right knee out. Open the right hip so the knee points out to the side.
  5. Place the right foot. Set the sole against the left ankle, the left calf, or the left inner thigh. Never against the side of the knee.
  6. Press both ways. Push the foot into the leg and the leg back into the foot with equal force. This mutual pressure is what makes the pose stable.
  7. Level the pelvis. Both hip points face forward. The lifted-leg hip wants to hike upward, so consciously let it settle down.
  8. Bring the hands together. Palms at the chest. Once that is steady, reach the arms overhead, shoulder-width apart.

Alignment cues

  • Foot above or below the knee, never on it. The side of the knee is not built to take sideways pressure.
  • Standing knee soft. Locking it makes small corrections impossible and the wobble worse.
  • Hip points forward and level. Opening the lifted knee out is good; letting the whole pelvis rotate with it is not.
  • Tailbone heavy. Do not let the front ribs push forward as the arms lift.
  • Toes spread. The foot on the floor should feel wide and awake, not gripping.

Breath pattern

  • Set the foot on an exhale, lift the arms on an inhale.
  • Breathe evenly through the nose. A held breath makes balance measurably worse.
  • When you wobble, exhale slowly rather than tensing. Most wobbles settle on their own.
  • Come out on an exhale, placing the foot down with control.

Suggested hold

  • First time: 15 to 20 seconds each side, near a wall.
  • Regular practice: 30 to 60 seconds each side.
  • Building steadiness: three rounds of 20 seconds per side is more useful than one long attempt.

Common mistakes

  • Foot on the side of the knee. The one genuine safety error in this pose. Move it above or below.
  • Hip hiking. The lifted-leg hip rides up and the torso tips. Let it drop and lengthen both sides of the waist equally.
  • Locking the standing knee. Feels stable for two seconds, then is not.
  • Moving eyes. Looking around, or at a moving screen, makes the balance far harder than it needs to be.
  • Holding the breath. Extremely common in balance shapes and reliably makes them worse.
  • Treating a wobble as failure. The corrections are the training. A perfectly still Tree Pose is doing less work than a slightly moving one.

What is actually happening

  • The muscles around the standing ankle make constant small adjustments, which is the main balance work in the pose.
  • The outer hip muscles of the standing leg work to keep the pelvis level over a single support point.
  • The lifted leg's hip rotates outward, which is a range that varies enormously between people and has almost nothing to do with practice time.
  • Your vestibular and visual systems and the position sense in your joints work together, which is why a fixed gaze helps so much.

Modifications

Support removes the fear of falling, and a body that is not braced against falling learns the shape faster.

Side to a wall

Stand with your lifted-leg side about six inches from a wall and rest that hand or elbow on it. You can lean as much or as little as you need, moment to moment. This is the version most people should start with and many should keep using.

Toes on the floor, heel on the ankle

Keep the toes of the lifted foot on the floor and rest only the heel against the standing ankle. Almost all the balance demand disappears while the hip rotation and the pelvis work stay exactly the same. This is called kickstand tree and it is a legitimate destination, not a waypoint.

Hand on a chair back

Place a sturdy chair in front of you and rest one or both hands on the back. This gives more support than a wall and lets you take the foot higher onto the inner thigh than you otherwise could.

Seated version

Sit tall on a chair with both feet flat. Bring the right ankle onto the left thigh just above the knee, letting the right knee open out to the side, and bring the palms together at the chest. You get the hip rotation and the upright spine with no balance component at all.

Foot on a block

If lifting the foot to the calf is not available, set a block on its tallest side next to the standing foot and rest the lifted foot on that instead. The hip still turns out, the balance stays manageable.

Hands at the chest, eyes open

Skip the arms overhead entirely. Reaching up raises your center of mass and makes the balance noticeably harder for no extra benefit while you are learning.

Progression

  • Away from the wall. Move six inches from the wall so it is there if you need it but you are not touching it.
  • Arms overhead. Reach up shoulder-width apart, keeping the ribs from flaring.
  • Longer holds. Work toward 60 seconds a side with the arms up.
  • Eyes closed. Only near a wall. Removing vision makes this dramatically harder and is genuinely useful balance training.
  • On an uneven surface. A folded blanket under the standing foot adds an unstable base. Advanced, and only when everything above is comfortable.

The next standing balance in the library is Warrior Three, which takes the same single-leg base into a horizontal reach.

Contraindications and care

Approach with care or use a wall or chair if: you have a balance disorder, inner-ear symptoms, low blood pressure, a history of falls, neuropathy or reduced sensation in the feet, a recent ankle, knee or hip injury, or you are pregnant, when balance shifts week to week.

Skip the standing version and use the seated one if: you are recovering from lower-limb or spinal surgery, or standing on one leg has been advised against for you.

Always practice within reach of something solid when balance is uncertain, and never place the lifted foot against the side of the standing knee. Work with a qualified teacher in person for hands-on guidance.
