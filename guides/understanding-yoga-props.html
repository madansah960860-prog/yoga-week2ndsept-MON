Understanding yoga props: what each one is actually for

There are only three jobs a prop can do. It can bring the floor closer to you, it can extend your reach, or it can hold weight so that you do not have to. Every block, strap, bolster, blanket, wedge and chair in a studio is doing one of those three things. Once you know which, choosing between them stops being a shopping decision and becomes a practice decision.

First, the idea that props are for beginners

This is worth dismantling before anything else, because it is the reason a lot of people practice worse than they need to.

In the Iyengar tradition, props are standard equipment for everyone in the room, including teachers with thirty years of practice. In restorative practice, props are not an accommodation, they are the entire method: without them there is no pose, because the whole intent is that nothing has to work. A person using two blocks and a strap in a seated forward fold is very often getting more out of the shape than a person reaching their toes with a rounded back and a held breath.

What a prop does is make the intent of a pose available to the body you actually have today. That is a different thing from making the pose easier, and sometimes a propped version is harder, because you can finally stay in it long enough for it to ask something of you.

Blocks: bringing the floor closer

If you buy one thing, buy two blocks. They change more poses more usefully than anything else.

A standard block is about 9 inches by 6 inches by 4 inches, which gives you three working heights depending on which face it rests on. Choosing between those three heights is the actual skill, and most people default to the lowest when the tallest would serve them better.

Under the hands in a standing fold. The point of Uttānāsana is folding at the hip joints with a long spine. If your hands only reach the floor by rounding your back, you have swapped a hip fold for a spinal flexion, which is a different pose. Two blocks at their tallest height under the hands restores the original shape.

Either side of the front foot in a lunge. Same principle. The hands have somewhere to land at a height that lets the chest stay open.

Under the sacrum in Bridge Pose. This one converts an active pose into a supported one. The hips rest on the block, nothing is holding you up, and a shape you could hold for forty-five seconds becomes one you can stay in for five minutes.

Between the thighs. Squeezed lightly, a block gives the inner thighs something to do and stops the knees splaying in Bridge or the pelvis feeling vague in Mountain Pose.

Under the knees in Bound Angle. Knees hanging unsupported in a seated hip shape produce a long slow strain. Blocks or firm cushions under them turn the same shape into something restful.

Material. Foam is light, forgiving, and correct for a first pair. Cork is heavier, firmer and does not compress under weight, which matters if you lean on it hard. Wood is the firmest and the least forgiving on a bare ankle. Buy foam.

Household substitute. Thick hardbacks taped together, or a shoebox packed solid. Fine under hands, never for standing on.

Straps: extending your reach

A strap is a flat woven belt, usually 6 or 8 feet, with a buckle. It exists so that you can hold a limb at a distance without hauling yourself toward it.

The clearest example is the reclining leg stretch, Supta Pādāṅguṣṭhāsana. Without a strap, most people grab behind the thigh and pull, which rounds the lower back and turns a leg stretch into a lower back stretch. With a strap around the foot, the arms hold at a distance, the back stays flat on the floor, and the shape becomes what it is supposed to be.

The same logic applies to seated forward folds, to any bind, and to shoulder work where the hands cannot reach each other behind the back. A strap held between two hands turns an impossible clasp into an adjustable one.

The less obvious use is as a boundary rather than an extension. Looped around both thighs above the knees and set to hip width, a strap stops the knees drifting apart in Bridge. Looped around both upper arms above the elbows, it stops the elbows splaying wide in Downward-Facing Dog or in a forearm shape.

Buying. Six feet is enough for most people and most uses. Eight feet if you are tall or want to make body loops. A D-ring buckle adjusts easily with one hand; a plastic cinch is secure but fiddlier to release.

Household substitute. A bathrobe belt, a long scarf, a dog leash, a luggage strap. Anything flat and non-stretchy. Avoid elastic, because the entire function depends on it not giving.

Blankets: height, padding and warmth

The most common seated modification in all of yoga is sitting on a folded blanket, and it is the fix for the most common seated complaint.

When you sit cross-legged on a flat floor, most people's pelvis tips backward, the lower back rounds, and staying upright becomes an effort held in the back muscles. Raising the hips two to four inches tips the pelvis slightly forward, the lower back regains its natural inward curve, and sitting stops being work. If cross-legged sitting is uncomfortable for you, this single change fixes it more often than anything else.

Beyond that: folded under the knees in a kneeling shape, rolled behind the knees to reduce how far they bend in Child's Pose, rolled under the ankles when the tops of the feet do not want to flatten, folded under the shoulders in Bridge so the neck has more room, and finally, over you in the last shape, because bodies cool quickly when they stop moving.

Buying. Firm and dense, not fluffy. A traditional Mexican-style cotton yoga blanket folds into a stable brick and holds its shape under weight. A fleece throw compresses to nothing and is only useful as a cover.

Household substitute. A folded bath towel, a firm cushion, or a couch cushion on its side.

Bolsters: holding the weight of you

A bolster is a firm stuffed cylinder or rectangle, around 25 inches long, and it is the defining prop of restorative practice. Its job is to support the torso so completely that no muscle has to hold anything, which is what makes a ten or twenty minute hold possible.

Firmness matters more than shape or size. A soft bolster compresses under your weight, stops supporting you, and quietly defeats the purpose. Rectangular bolsters are more stable and stack better; round ones give a higher, domed support that suits chest-opening shapes.

The uses that matter most: lengthwise between the thighs in a supported Child's Pose so the whole front of the body rests along it, under the thighs and knees in reclining bound angle, and under the knees in the final resting shape, where it lets the lower back settle.

Household substitute. Two firm bed pillows stacked and pressed into a line, a rolled duvet, or a couch cushion with a folded blanket on top. These work genuinely well and there is no reason to buy a bolster until you know you like long supported holds. Our Evening Wind-Down sequence is written to work with pillows.

Wedges: a small prop with one specific job

A wedge is a foam ramp, usually about 20 inches wide and tapering from around 2 inches to nothing. It has one main job and does it well: placed under the heels of the hands, it reduces the angle at the wrist in any shape where you bear weight on the hands.

If wrist extension is uncomfortable for you, a wedge is worth more than another block. It is also useful under the heels in a squat, where limited ankle range otherwise tips you backward.

This is a specialist purchase and most people never need one. A rolled hand towel under the heels of the hands does a rough version of the same thing.

The chair: the most under-rated prop there is

A chair is a stable surface at seat height that you can press into, sit on, sit sideways on, and rest limbs against. It is not a concession to limitation. It is a tool that makes a large part of the practice available at a different altitude.

Three things it does that nothing else does. It gives you a waist-height landing place for the hands in standing shapes. It supports the thigh in a lunge or a warrior shape so the leg carries less load and you can actually study the alignment for a full minute. And it lets you build an entire practice sitting down, which our Desk Reset does in eight minutes.

Which chair. No wheels, no swivel, flat firm seat, arms either absent or low enough to stay out of the way. Put it against a wall for anything you press into.

The wall and the floor

Both free, both already installed, and between them they cover more than any purchase.

A wall gives you a straight immovable reference, which your own sense of body position is surprisingly bad at supplying. Behind you it shows you where your spine is. Beside you it makes balances studyable. In front of you it takes the wrists out of Downward-Facing Dog. Underneath you it gives you Legs Up the Wall for the cost of moving a side table.

The floor itself is a prop in the shapes where you rest on it: forehead down in Child's Pose, forearms down in Sphinx. Noticing what the floor is giving you is part of learning to notice what a prop gives you.

The buying order, and where to stop

  1. Two foam blocks. The highest return of anything on this list.
  2. One strap, six feet. Cheap, small, opens up seated and reclining leg work.
  3. A mat, if your floor is hard, cold or slippery. On carpet, wait.
  4. A firm blanket. Under the hips, under the knees, over you at the end.
  5. A bolster, only if long supported holds turn out to be something you actually do.

That is the whole list. Wheels, sandbags, straps with three buckles, inflatable cushions and specialized wedges all exist and all have their uses in specific traditions, but none of them belongs in a first year of practice.

How to tell you need a prop

Three questions, asked while you are in the shape. Can you breathe evenly? Is your spine doing what the pose intends, rather than rounding to make up the difference? Could you stay here for the suggested hold time? A no to any of them means reach for something.

A yes to all three does not mean you should not use a prop anyway. Some shapes are simply more informative supported, and finding out which ones is a large part of what a practice teaches you.

Using props safely

Do not stand on foam blocks. Place a chair against a wall for anything you press into. Check that a cushion stack cannot slide before putting weight on it. Clear the wall and the floor before Legs Up the Wall.
